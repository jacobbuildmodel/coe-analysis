In January 2021, MAS's monthly average rate was S$1.278 for 100 yen, so a
S$1,000 trip budget bought about 78,200 yen. In December 2025 it was S$0.829,
and the same budget bought about 120,600 yen. Tokyo did get cheaper. Did
Singapore get richer? Its dollar did rise, but most of the change was the
yen's own fall.

It held beyond these five years. Of the 46 five-year stretches since
2005 in which the Singapore dollar gained on the yen, the yen's own fall was
the larger part in 39 (added after outside review, not sealed). Over the whole
two decades, though, the Singapore dollar's own slow climb added up to more.

Who moved is not who benefits: whichever currency moved, a Singaporean's
dollar bought the full gain in yen.

At these endpoints, single months give 54 per cent, so the tests average three
months at each end. On that measure, from early 2021 to late 2025, the
Singapore dollar rose 49.5 per cent against the yen; its broad index, its
value against all its trading partners, rose 13 per cent; the yen's broad
index fell 25 per cent. I sealed five bets on the split on an
[open question page](/open/2026-10-03-singapore-dollar/) before opening the
data. Four held. The one I expected to fail, on the ringgit, failed.

## The argument: strength, delivered through steering

The comfortable story says a strong economy makes a strong currency. But
since 1981 MAS has steered the Singapore dollar inside an unpublished band,
which, its framework FAQ says, crawls "at different rates over time,
depending on current and future expected price developments in the economy";
MAS eased in 2015 and 2016 as "the growth and inflation outlook softened".
The long-run rise of such a currency is commonly linked to productivity
growth (Balassa 1964; Samuelson 1964) and to an anti-inflation stance. So
steering sets the speed and fundamentals shape the direction: strength,
delivered through steering. That is theory, not a finding here.

A rate against one currency is a ratio, so it moves when either side moves.
Telling who moved means measuring each side against everyone else.

## The extremes: a passenger and a boat

Hong Kong's dollar, held between HK$7.75 and HK$7.85 per US dollar, is a
passenger in the US dollar's car. The yen, floating since 1973, is a boat
without an anchor. Singapore is on cruise control inside a lane, with MAS
resetting the speed at each review.

## My bets: five predictions, sealed before the data

The Bank for International Settlements publishes each currency's broad index
against 64 economies' currencies, weighted by trade, so a rise against the yen
splits into the Singapore dollar rising, the yen falling, and a remainder.

1. **The split adds up** (55 per cent): the remainder within 20 per cent of the
   move, and BIS rates within half a per cent of MAS's.
2. **The yen's share** (90 per cent): at least half the rise against the yen
   was the yen falling, and more of it than the Singapore dollar's own rise.
3. **The ringgit's share** (8 per cent): the same, for the ringgit.
4. **The slow path** (80 per cent): the steadiest or second steadiest broad
   index of 11 currencies: the Singapore dollar, yen, ringgit, won, renminbi,
   baht, rupiah, US dollar, euro, Australian dollar and Hong Kong dollar.
5. **Policy, not growth** (35 per cent): its path lined up clearly better with
   MAS's decisions than with growth.

## What the data said: nearly three-quarters was the yen's fall

![For the yen and the ringgit, three moves in per cent from early 2021 to late 2025: the Singapore dollar against everyone, the partner against everyone, and the Singapore dollar against the partner](/figs/sgd_chart1_split.svg)

Of the Singapore dollar's 49.5 per cent rise against the yen, 73 per cent was
the yen falling against everyone and 31 per cent was the Singapore dollar
rising against everyone; the remainder was 4 per cent the other way. These are
shares of the log change, in which the three parts add up exactly. The bet
held. So did the first: the split added up, with the ringgit's
remainder at 17 per cent of the move, and BIS rates within a tenth of a per
cent of MAS's own.

Against the US dollar instead of everyone, the yen's share was even larger,
93 per cent, while the ringgit's split depended on the yardstick: against the
US dollar the ringgit fell 2.1 per cent, 45 per cent of the move (added after
outside review, not sealed).

## Five-year stretches: mostly the yen, but not over two decades

![Every five-year window since August 2005: the Singapore dollar's own rise and the yen's own fall as shares of the Singapore dollar's rise against the yen, with the windows where it did not rise shaded](/figs/sgd_chart4_rolling.svg)

The chart splits every five-year window from August 2005, in three-month
steps (added after outside review, not sealed). From August 2005 to the
latest month, though, the Singapore dollar rose 87 per cent against
the yen, its own rise 57 per cent of the move and the yen's fall 51. The
yen's falls and recoveries partly offset; the Singapore dollar's climb kept
adding up. That long split was sealed as context.

## What failed, as I expected: the ringgit rose against everyone

I gave the ringgit bet 8 per cent, expecting the strong ringgit of 2024 and
2025 to end the window higher against everyone. It did. The Singapore dollar
rose 13 per cent against everyone and the ringgit about 9 per cent, so the
Singapore dollar gained only 5 per cent on the ringgit, and its own climb did
the work. That is two sides to every rate: against the yen, the yen moved
most; against the ringgit, the Singapore dollar.

**The tally.** 4 of 5 held against the 2.68 I expected. The Brier score, how
far each confidence sat from what happened, was 0.136, against 0.25 for a
forecaster who always says 50 per cent; lower is better.

## The obvious objection: isn't steering just strength?

MAS's slope partly tracks the economy, so a path that follows MAS might
follow growth one step removed. Over the 61 stretches between MAS's
decisions, the index's path and MAS's decisions ranked them in a partly
similar order: a rank correlation of 0.47, where 1 is the same order and 0 is
no relation. With growth it was 0.11.
MAS's lead over growth was 0.35, against a pass line of 0.20; allowing for
chance, a 90 per cent range put it between 0.11 and 0.59. The bet held, at 35
per cent, and a finer coding of MAS's slope gave the same answer, a lead of
0.346. A correlation of only 0.47 also means the band left real room to move.

## Where it flips: inflation lined up as well as MAS did

With CPI inflation in place of growth the result turns over: the rank
correlation with inflation was 0.51, and MAS's lead -0.04. That was a sealed
sensitivity, not scored. Beside it sits the sentence sealed with the bet:

> The design leans toward the rival. The band is the path MAS keeps the index
> on, so a score that describes the band will match the index's path wherever
> MAS held the index inside it. A result that the path followed MAS's
> decisions is weak evidence that MAS rather than the economy moved the
> Singapore dollar. A result that it followed growth at least as closely is
> strong evidence against the rival.

## The world view: steadier than Hong Kong's broad index, and all the others

![The typical monthly move of each currency's broad index since August 2005, eleven currencies ranked from steadiest to least steady](/figs/sgd_chart2_steady.svg)

The window starts in August 2005, after the ringgit's and the renminbi's pegs
to the US dollar ended in July 2005. Since then the Singapore dollar's broad
index moved about 0.47 per cent in a typical month (the standard deviation of
the monthly change), the steadiest of the 11. The bet held. Hong Kong's came
second, at 1.00 per cent, about 0.78 of the US dollar's swing, and the two
moved together with a correlation of 0.92 on monthly changes. The yen was the
least steady, at 2.16 per cent.

## The MAS path: a slow line with turns at the decisions

The BIS index moved with MAS's own with a correlation of 0.92, also on
monthly changes, so it describes the index MAS steers.

![The Singapore dollar's broad index since 2001, with each MAS decision marked beneath and GDP growth over each stretch between decisions](/figs/sgd_chart3_path.svg)

The index climbed slowly while MAS kept a rising slope, went flat or dipped
after MAS eased in 2009 and 2020, and climbed steepest in 2022, when MAS kept
the band rising and lifted the whole band at once.

## What would change my mind

- A later five-year window in which the Singapore dollar's own rise
  outweighed the yen's fall.
- A broad index that swung like a floating currency's.
- A path that lined up with growth as closely as with MAS's decisions.

## The verdict: mostly the yen; the path lined up with MAS

Most of the Singapore dollar's rise against the yen was the yen falling against
everyone; most of its rise against the ringgit was the Singapore dollar rising
against everyone; its broad path followed MAS's decisions more closely than
growth.

With CPI inflation in place of growth, MAS's lead was -0.04, and -0.01 with
inflation a quarter ahead (added after outside review, not sealed). So the
record cannot separate MAS's decisions from the inflation MAS targets.

Weaker than they read:

- **The ringgit:** the two countries weigh heavily in each other's baskets,
  so the result was sealed as weaker evidence than the yen's.
- **The policy result:** sealed as weak evidence, since the band is drawn
  around the path.
- **The yen:** true of the recent window and most five-year stretches, but
  over two decades the Singapore dollar's own rise was larger.

## What it changes: the cheap yen was mostly Japan's story

The evidence cut against the comfortable story's second half: in most
five-year stretches, a cheaper Tokyo was mostly a weaker yen rather than a
stronger Singapore dollar. It helped the steering story less than it seems,
since inflation matched the path as well as MAS did. Anyone weighing
"strong economy, strong dollar" now has to count the yen's own swings, which
dominated most five-year stretches; the Singapore dollar's steady climb, which
dominated two decades; and the gain in yen, which went to Singaporeans
whoever moved.

## What this does not explain: prices, flows and motive

Japan's consumer prices rose 13 per cent over the window, so in Tokyo prices
the gain was smaller than the rate's; adjusted for both countries' prices, the
yen's own fall was still 71 per cent of the real rise (added after outside
review, not sealed). Nothing here covers interest rates, capital flows, or why
MAS set the slopes it set. I knew the yen had fallen since 2021 when the
window's rule was set, and disclosed it with the sealed bets.
